## Large and medium land mammals

White-tailed Deer  
Odocoileus virginianus  

Native  

Description  
White-tailed deer are medium-sized, slender-bodied deer with a long tail that flashes bright white underneath when alarmed—often the first clear “field mark” a visitor notices. In summer, their coat is more reddish-brown; in winter it shifts to a grayer fawn tone. Adult males (bucks) grow antlers with points branching from a main beam, while females usually have none. Tracks are heart-shaped with two sharp hooves; in soft ground you may also see dewclaw marks behind. citeturn5view2  

Deer growth and seasonal behavior are closely tied to food and breeding cycles: bucks shed antlers annually, and breeding activity (the rut) peaks in fall to early winter with timing that varies by region. Deer are crepuscular—most active near sunrise and sunset—so visitors often see them moving quietly between cover and feeding areas at those times. citeturn5view2  

Habitat  
In Central Texas, white-tailed deer thrive anywhere brushy cover and woodland edges provide both hiding places and nearby forage. They rest during the day in thickets or protected spots and often feed into the night when conditions are calm. Their home ranges are typically small when food and water are reliable, which is why the same neighborhood greenbelt can host the same individuals for months or years. citeturn5view2  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for deer along brushy trail edges, woodland openings, and quiet stretches of riparian corridor—especially at sunrise and the hour before dark, when they step into open lawns, meadows, or mowed clearings to graze. citeturn5view2  

Ecological Role  
As abundant herbivores, deer shape plant communities through browsing and grazing. Where deer numbers are high, they can reduce regeneration of some native shrubs and trees and shift what plant species dominate understories and wildflower patches. They also move nutrients across the landscape as they travel between bedding cover and feeding areas. citeturn5view2  
- Influences which native plants successfully regenerate  
- Provides prey (especially fawns and carrion) for predators and scavengers  
- Moves seeds and nutrients across habitats through feeding and droppings citeturn5view2  

Fun Fact  
- Antler point-counts are not a reliable way to tell a buck’s age; nutrition and genetics strongly affect antler size. citeturn5view2  
- Deer are often easiest to spot in the hour before dark because activity rises just before sunset. citeturn5view2  

Coyote  
Canis latrans  

Native  

Description  
Coyotes are medium-sized, doglike carnivores with a slender build, pointed ears, a narrow muzzle, and a bushy tail often tipped darker. Their coat typically looks gray-buff with black “peppering,” blending into dry grasses, leaf litter, and brush. In the field, you may glimpse just a smooth, low, trotting silhouette crossing a trail corridor. Tracks resemble a small dog’s but tend to be more oval and direct in travel lines, reflecting their efficient movement. citeturn5view0  

Coyotes are highly adaptable in diet and behavior, which is a big reason they persist in and around cities. Their food changes with season and opportunity—rodents and rabbits are important, but they will also eat fruit and carrion and take advantage of human-altered landscapes. This flexibility helps coyotes occupy everything from big preserves to smaller neighborhood greenbelts. citeturn5view0  

Habitat  
In Central Texas parks, coyotes use a patchwork of habitats: brushy edges, creek corridors, open fields for hunting, and dense cover for daytime shelter. They can cross developed areas using “green threads” like drainage easements and riparian strips. Because they move widely, they may be present even if rarely seen—often detected instead by tracks, scat, or nighttime vocalizations. citeturn5view0  

Where Found In The Park  
Coyotes most often travel along quiet, linear routes—creekside trails, utility corridors, and the transition zone where woodland meets meadow. Early morning and late evening are prime viewing times, though they may also move during daylight on cool, overcast days. citeturn5view0  

Ecological Role  
Coyotes function as top predators in many Central Texas urban-wildland mosaics. By hunting rodents and rabbits and scavenging carcasses, they influence prey populations and help recycle nutrients. Importantly, predator removal can sometimes increase conflict by opening territory to younger dispersing coyotes, which may behave differently than established adults. citeturn5view0  
- Helps regulate rodent and rabbit populations  
- Scavenges carrion, accelerating nutrient recycling  
- Shapes wildlife behavior (prey may alter foraging to avoid predation risk) citeturn5view0  

Fun Fact  
- Coyotes can shift their diet dramatically by season—more carrion in winter, more rodents and fruit in warmer months. citeturn5view0  
- They are one of North America’s most successful “urban adapters,” routinely using cities as habitat. citeturn5view0  

Bobcat  
Lynx rufus  

Native  

Description  
Bobcats are compact wild cats with a short “bobbed” tail—black on top at the tip and paler below—plus tufted ears and a facial ruff that can make the head look broad. Their coat is reddish-brown to grayish with black streaking and spots; the back of each ear is notably dark with a contrasting pale patch. Most sightings are brief: a low, silent animal slipping into cover, often at dusk or at night. citeturn11view1  

Bobcats are active year-round and do not hibernate. They hunt primarily by stalking and pouncing, relying on sharp hearing and vision. In Central Texas, rabbits are a key food source, with other prey—including rodents and squirrels—supplementing as available. Their ability to use agricultural and urban edges (where cover remains) helps them persist near people. citeturn11view1  

Habitat  
Bobcats prefer areas with “broken” terrain that provides cover and hunting opportunities: rocky limestone hillsides, brushy draws, woodland edges, and creek corridors. They rest during the day in thickets, hollow trees, or rocky crevices. Trails and unimproved roads can become travel corridors, and bobcats sometimes hunt along them—one reason why early-morning hikers occasionally get lucky. citeturn11view1  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for bobcats where dense cover meets open hunting ground: brushy trail bends, rocky slopes near canyons, and quieter greenbelt stretches with good rabbit habitat. If you see one, it’s often at the edge of daylight—just after sunset or before sunrise. citeturn11view1  

Ecological Role  
As specialized predators of rabbits and small mammals, bobcats help balance prey populations and can reduce over-browsing pressure indirectly by limiting herbivore numbers. They also contribute to healthy scavenger communities when they leave remains that smaller animals and insects then consume. citeturn11view1  
- Helps regulate rabbits, rodents, and squirrels  
- Encourages prey species to use cover strategically, shaping habitat use  
- Supports scavengers through leftover prey remains citeturn11view1  

Fun Fact  
- Bobcats often hunt from trails and roads because these linear openings can concentrate prey movement. citeturn11view1  
- Their tail tip pattern—black above, pale below—is a useful ID mark in a fast glimpse. citeturn11view1  

Common Gray Fox  
Urocyon cinereoargenteus  

Native  

Description  
Gray foxes are medium-sized foxes with “salt-and-pepper” gray upperparts, reddish-brown legs, and a distinct black stripe along the top of the tail ending in a black tip. Unlike red foxes, gray foxes lack a white tail tip—an important quick ID feature. Their face often shows darker patches along the sides of the muzzle and lower jaw, and their overall look is more grizzled than bright. citeturn11view0  

A unique trait sets gray foxes apart from most canids: they are adept climbers. They can climb trees to escape danger, rest, or even forage, using curved claws and athletic movements that can surprise observers who expect “foxes stay on the ground.” Their diet shifts seasonally and includes small mammals, birds, insects, and plant foods such as fruit and acorns. citeturn11view0  

Habitat  
Gray foxes do especially well in edge habitats—places where woodland meets open areas—often benefiting from the patchwork created by trails, small clearings, and suburban development. They den in brush piles, rocky holes, hollow trees (sometimes above ground), and other sheltered sites, usually not far from water. Their tolerance for urban settings makes them a realistic resident even in fragmented greenbelts. citeturn11view0  

Where Found In The Park  
Gray foxes are most likely along woodland edges, brushy ravines, and dense understory near water. Watch quietly at dusk near thickets bordering open fields—foxes often use trails and old roads as travel lanes, then veer into cover. citeturn11view0  

Ecological Role  
Gray foxes are mid-level predators that connect multiple parts of the food web. By eating rodents and rabbits, they help regulate small-mammal populations; by eating fruit and occasionally acorns, they may disperse seeds across habitat edges. They also serve as prey or competitors for larger predators such as coyotes and bobcats. citeturn11view0  
- Controls some small-mammal and insect populations  
- Can disperse seeds through fruit consumption  
- Serves as prey/competitor within predator communities citeturn11view0  

Fun Fact  
- Gray foxes can climb trees—an uncommon ability among wild canids. citeturn11view0  
- The black stripe on the top of the tail is a reliable field mark in a quick sighting. citeturn11view0  

Northern Raccoon  
Procyon lotor  

Native  

Description  
Raccoons are robust, medium-sized mammals famous for a black facial “mask” and a bushy tail ringed with alternating dark and light bands. Their coarse grayish fur often looks slightly “salted” with darker guard hairs. In parks, raccoons are commonly seen at night near water, or detected by their hand-like tracks in mud along creek edges. citeturn5view1  

Young raccoons resemble adults but have woollier fur. Raccoons are opportunistic omnivores with strong climbing ability and dexterous forepaws, allowing them to open containers and manipulate food—one reason they thrive in urban settings. In Texas, they are described as common and well adapted to human-altered environments. citeturn5view1  

Habitat  
Raccoons occur statewide in Texas and readily use riparian corridors, woodlands, and urban green spaces. Den sites include hollow trees, logs, and sometimes attics or structures. Where food is abundant—natural sources like crayfish and fruit, or human sources like unsecured trash—raccoon numbers can rise. citeturn5view1turn10view0  

Where Found In The Park  
Expect raccoons along creeks, ponds, and drainage channels, especially where trees overhang water and provide den cavities. At night, check muddy shorelines for tracks and look for eye-shine in a flashlight beam (without disturbing the animal). citeturn5view1  

Ecological Role  
Raccoons are omnivores and scavengers that move energy through the ecosystem by feeding on invertebrates, fruit, and small animals. They recycle nutrients by consuming carrion and can influence nesting success of some birds and turtles through egg predation. Their adaptability makes them a strong “urban wildlife” indicator species. citeturn5view1  
- Consumes aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, helping cycle nutrients  
- Disperses some plant seeds via fruit consumption  
- Provides prey for larger predators and supports scavenger networks citeturn5view1  

Fun Fact  
- Raccoons walk with the whole hind foot touching the ground—an unusual gait feature noted in field descriptions. citeturn5view1  
- Their dexterous paws help them exploit both natural foods and human food sources, contributing to their success near cities. citeturn5view1  

Virginia Opossum  
Didelphis virginiana  

Native  

Description  
Virginia opossums are about the size of a small terrier, with a long, scaly, prehensile tail, a pointed snout, and dark, leathery ears. Their coat can appear grayish or darker depending on color phase, and their feet include an opposable, thumb-like big toe on the hind foot (without a claw). Females develop a pouch during breeding season for carrying young—one of the most distinctive life-history traits among mammals in the region. citeturn10view0  

Opossums are highly flexible in denning and diet. They often use hollow trees and logs but will also shelter under buildings, in woodpiles, rock piles, and burrows dug by other animals. Their stomach-content studies in Texas demonstrate an omnivorous diet heavy in insects but also including small vertebrates and seasonal fruit. citeturn10view0  

Habitat  
Opossums favor deciduous woodlands and riparian corridors, but in Central Texas they readily use prairies, farmlands, neighborhoods, and parks—especially where woody cover follows creeks. They move through “connected” habitat—tree lines and brushy ditches—that allow them to cross otherwise open or developed areas. citeturn10view0  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for opossums after dark along creekside trails, near dumpsters or picnic areas (where food scent lingers), and around brush piles or hollow logs. Daytime sightings are less common because they typically rest in sheltered dens. citeturn10view0  

Ecological Role  
Opossums act as omnivorous scavengers and insect-eaters, helping break down organic waste and moving nutrients through the food web. They also provide prey for larger predators and owls, and their use of many den types makes them a “habitat generalist” linking multiple park microhabitats. citeturn10view0  
- Consumes large quantities of insects and other invertebrates  
- Scavenges carrion and human food waste, accelerating decomposition pathways  
- Serves as prey for coyotes, owls, and other predators citeturn10view0  

Fun Fact  
- Opossums often reuse burrows made by other animals because they are not expert diggers. citeturn10view0  
- Their tail is prehensile (gripping), which helps with climbing and balance. citeturn10view0  

Nine-banded Armadillo  
Dasypus novemcinctus  

Native  

Description  
Nine-banded armadillos are unmistakable: their back and sides are protected by a bony “armor” (carapace) with large shoulder and rump shields and nine flexible bands between. They have strong digging claws—especially on the front feet—and a long, tapering tail ringed with bony plates. Color is typically brownish with scattered pale hairs, and their peg-like teeth reflect a diet focused on invertebrates rather than chewing tough plant matter. citeturn10view1  

Armadillos are built for digging and foraging by smell, often rooting in soil and leaf litter at night. They are common in central and eastern Texas and are strongly influenced by soil characteristics: areas with more easily dug soils tend to support higher densities. Many park visitors notice them as “moving mounds,” heard rustling in leaves before they’re seen. citeturn10view1  

Habitat  
In Central Texas, armadillos use woodlands, brushy edges, and riparian corridors where soils allow burrow construction and where invertebrates are plentiful. Soil texture is particularly important; compacted clay or rocky ground can reduce burrow opportunities, while friable soils and leaf litter support both digging and prey availability. citeturn10view1  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for armadillos at dusk or night along leaf-littered trail edges, under oak canopies, and near creek bottoms where moist soils support insects. Fresh digging—small cone-shaped holes and disturbed soil—often reveals recent feeding activity. citeturn10view1  

Ecological Role  
Armadillos are ecosystem “soil stirrers.” Their foraging disturbs the ground surface, which can help mix organic material into soil but may also be disruptive when concentrated. They also create burrows that other wildlife may use for shelter, and they serve as prey or scavenged carrion for larger animals. citeturn10view1  
- Aerates and mixes soil through digging and foraging  
- Creates burrows that can become shelter for other species  
- Supports predator and scavenger food webs as prey/carcasses citeturn10view1  

Fun Fact  
- Soil that is easier to dig can support more armadillos—soil texture measurably affects their local abundance. citeturn10view1  
- Their armor is bone covered by skin, not a shell like a turtle’s—so it flexes with those “bands.” citeturn10view1

Striped Skunk  
Mephitis mephitis  

Native  

Description  
Striped skunks are stout-bodied mammals with bold black-and-white striping that usually forms two white stripes along the back merging near the neck, plus a long, bushy tail. Pattern variation is common, but the high-contrast coloration is always a warning sign. Skunks have powerful scent glands that can spray a strong musk when threatened, typically after clear warning behaviors like stamping or posturing. citeturn10view2  

They are common across Texas and can adapt well to urban conditions. Because they are primarily nocturnal, park visitors most often encounter skunks at dusk, near picnic areas, or crossing quiet roads. citeturn10view2  

Habitat  
Striped skunks use a wide range of habitats, from woodlands and grasslands to neighborhoods and park edges. They den in protected spaces—burrows, brush piles, and under structures—often close to reliable food and water. Local outbreaks of diseases like rabies and distemper can cause temporary population declines, but skunks often recover afterward. citeturn10view2  

Where Found In The Park  
Expect skunks near edges: where mowed lawns meet taller grass, around brush piles, and along creek corridors with insect-rich soils. In the evening, scan trail edges for slow-moving, low silhouettes and listen for rustling in leaf litter. citeturn10view2  

Ecological Role  
Striped skunks help control many ground-dwelling invertebrates and small vertebrates and also act as scavengers. Their presence near urban parks can reduce some pest insects, but their adaptability also means they may come into conflict with people where food attractants are available. citeturn10view2  
- Helps regulate insects and other invertebrates  
- Scavenges carrion, supporting nutrient cycling  
- Serves as prey/carcasses for larger predators and scavengers citeturn10view2  

Fun Fact  
- Skunks usually give clear warning signals (stamping, posturing) before spraying—backing away calmly can prevent a spray incident. citeturn10view2  
- Striping patterns vary—no two skunks look exactly the same in stripe shape. citeturn10view2  

Ringtail  
Bassariscus astutus  

Native  

Description  
Ringtails are cat-sized carnivores with a long, raccoon-like tail marked by many alternating black-and-white rings and a dark tip. They have a slim body, pointed muzzle, and a distinctive face with pale patches around the eyes and ears. Their build is agile, suited for climbing and navigating rocky ledges, and their dramatic tail is often the clearest ID feature if you catch a glimpse at night. citeturn5view3  

Ringtails are typically nocturnal and secretive, so they are rarely seen even when present. Signs may include tracks near rocky water crossings or sightings around dusk near cliffs, boulder piles, or old structures where they can den. citeturn5view3  

Habitat  
In the Central Texas Hill Country context, ringtails are closely associated with rocky terrain, crevices, and sheltered den sites. They use cliffs, rock piles, and sometimes human structures for cover—especially where these features sit near water or productive foraging areas. Their ability to exploit complex terrain makes canyonlands and limestone breaks particularly suitable. citeturn5view3turn31search6  

Where Found In The Park  
Look near rocky ledges, limestone outcrops, and shaded canyon-like trail segments—especially where those features sit near a creek or spring-fed corridor. Nighttime is when ringtails move most, so purposeful dusk “quiet walks” are the best chance. citeturn5view3  

Ecological Role  
As small predators and omnivores, ringtails help regulate rodents and other small animals and contribute to scavenging. In rocky ecosystems, they are part of a specialized community that depends on crevices and ledges—habitats that also shelter reptiles, bats, and many invertebrates. citeturn5view3  
- Helps control small rodents in rocky habitats  
- Connects rock-shelter food webs through hunting and scavenging  
- Indicates intact rocky cover and denning structure citeturn5view3  

Fun Fact  
- The tail can show 14–16 alternating rings—one of the most distinctive mammal markings in the region. citeturn5view3  
- Ringtails are excellent climbers, well adapted to limestone ledges and crevice-rich terrain. citeturn5view3  

American Badger  
Taxidea taxus  

Native  

Description  
American badgers are robust, low-slung mammals with powerful front legs and very long front claws built for digging. They have grizzled gray-yellow fur, a bold white stripe running from the nose up over the head, and dark facial patches that give them a high-contrast “masked” look. Because they spend substantial time underground, many visitors never see the animal itself—but may notice large soil mounds or excavation sites. citeturn10view3  

Badgers depend heavily on burrowing prey such as ground squirrels and similar animals. Their digging behavior is efficient and forceful, and they can open up substantial holes quickly. In Texas, they occur across much of the state except the extreme east and may be expanding eastward in association with land clearing and grassland creation. citeturn10view3  

Habitat  
Badgers are most common in open habitats—prairies, grasslands, and brushy rangeland—where digging is feasible and burrowing prey is available. In park settings, they are more likely in open preserves and large tracts with intact ground-layer habitat, rather than high-traffic, heavily landscaped urban parks. citeturn10view3  

Where Found In The Park  
Badgers are most likely in open grasslands and broad meadow systems with sandy-to-loamy soils suitable for digging. Look for fresh excavations near prairie edges or less-traveled service roads, especially in large natural areas with minimal nightly disturbance. citeturn10view3  

Ecological Role  
Badgers are major “soil ecosystem engineers” through digging. Their excavations can alter microhabitats, create shelter used by other animals, and influence prey distributions. They also help regulate populations of burrowing rodents, which can shape vegetation patterns indirectly. citeturn10view3  
- Controls burrowing rodents and similar prey  
- Creates and modifies burrows that other wildlife may reuse  
- Mixes and aerates soil through excavation activity citeturn10view3  

Fun Fact  
- Badgers are closely tied to the distribution of burrowing prey—where ground squirrels thrive, badgers often follow. citeturn10view3  
- Their front claws can exceed 25 mm in length, reflecting their specialized digging lifestyle. citeturn10view3  

## Small mammals and burrowers

Eastern Fox Squirrel  
Sciurus niger  

Native  

Description  
Fox squirrels are large tree squirrels with reddish or rusty underparts and brownish to grayish upperparts. Their tail is cinnamon mixed with black, and their overall size is noticeably bigger than many other squirrels seen in neighborhood trees. They’re often relaxed in open, park-like woodlands—sitting upright to watch trails, bounding across lawns, or raiding acorn-producing oaks. citeturn26view0  

They use both hollow trees and leafy nests, and their seasonal routine tracks mast availability: acorns and nuts dominate fall and winter diets, with more fruits, green shoots, and insects in warmer months. They cache nuts in individual buried stores and can relocate many by smell—an important behavior for both squirrel survival and seed movement. citeturn26view0  

Habitat  
Fox squirrels do well in mature mixed woodlands and bottomland riparian corridors—especially where oak, pecan, walnut, and other food trees occur. In Central Texas, live-oak motts and wooded creek corridors provide classic habitat. During drought, access to water can matter, and squirrels may concentrate near dependable water or succulent foods. citeturn26view0  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for fox squirrels in tall, mature trees along creeks, in older oak stands, and in “park-like” groves with open understory. They are often visible in daytime, especially mid-morning, moving between nut trees and den trees. citeturn26view0  

Ecological Role  
Fox squirrels are key mast consumers and seed movers. By burying nuts and failing to retrieve every cache, they can contribute to the dispersal of oaks and other nut-bearing trees. They also serve as important prey for bobcats, hawks, and other predators. citeturn26view0turn11view1  
- Disperses and “plants” nuts through caching behavior  
- Provides prey for raptors and mammalian predators  
- Helps cycle nutrients via mast consumption and waste citeturn26view0  

Fun Fact  
- Fox squirrels locate buried nuts partly by smell, even when caches are scattered. citeturn26view0  
- In Texas, fox squirrels can thrive in surprisingly urban settings when mature trees and corridors remain. citeturn26view0  

Eastern Gray Squirrel  
Sciurus carolinensis  

Native / Introduced (locally)  

Description  
Eastern gray squirrels are medium-sized squirrels with gray-toned fur and a tail whose hairs often show dark bands tipped with white. In winter, a pale spot at the base of the ear can stand out. In Central Texas cities and older neighborhoods, gray squirrels may be locally common, including in urban parks where they were introduced beyond their original Texas range. citeturn26view1  

They den in hollow trees when available but also build leafy nests lined with shredded bark or plant fibers. Gray squirrels are active during the day, and visitors most often see them running along branches, pausing to bark, or carrying food to den trees. citeturn26view1  

Habitat  
In Texas, gray squirrels are strongly associated with dense bottomland and swampy hardwood communities in their native range, but they can persist in urban areas wherever mature trees provide cavities, canopy connectivity, and reliable food such as acorns and pecans. Habitat reduction has historically been an issue, but urban plantings can create “islands” of suitable habitat. citeturn26view1  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for gray squirrels in thick groves of mature trees—especially where canopies connect over trails and where there are plenty of hollow trunks or large limbs. They are most visible in morning and late afternoon, often foraging on the ground under nut-bearing trees. citeturn26view1  

Ecological Role  
Gray squirrels are important consumers and dispersers of tree seeds and can influence which trees regenerate in a park’s understory. They also contribute to food webs as prey for hawks and mammalian predators and as secondary cavity users (and creators, indirectly, by selecting cavity-rich trees). citeturn26view1turn11view1  
- Moves acorns and nuts, supporting forest regeneration  
- Provides prey for raptors and predators  
- Links canopy and ground-layer food webs through foraging citeturn26view1  

Fun Fact  
- Gray squirrels often build nests with two openings—useful for quick escape. citeturn26view1  
- Many Texas urban populations exist where the species was introduced outside its native in-state range. citeturn26view1  

Rock Squirrel  
Otospermophilus variegatus  

Native  

Description  
Rock squirrels are large ground squirrels with mottled gray-brown fur and a fairly bushy tail edged with pale hairs. In some parts of Texas, the head or upper back can appear darker. They often sit upright on rocks, walls, or ledges to scan for danger—one of the most recognizable “ground squirrel postures” a visitor will see. citeturn26view2  

Rock squirrels live in colonies and use burrows for shelter and raising young. They are active in daylight, making them relatively easy to observe compared with many nocturnal mammals. Their social structure often centers on maternal groups with a dominant male, especially during breeding season. citeturn26view2  

Habitat  
In Texas, rock squirrels occur in central regions and are closely linked to rocky terrain—outcrops, canyon slopes, and limestone breaks—where burrows can be anchored among rocks. ## Invasive and introduced mammals

Feral Hog  
Sus scrofa  

Invasive / Introduced  

Description  
Feral hogs vary widely in appearance because Texas populations include feral domestic hogs, Eurasian wild boar, and hybrids. Wild boar traits can include darker coloration, grizzled guard hairs, a dorsal mane, and a straight tail with a tuft. Hogs are powerfully built with strong snouts for rooting, and their sign is often more obvious than the animals: torn-up turf, churned creek banks, wallows, and muddy tracks. citeturn33view3  

They are now widespread across Texas, occupying nearly all of the state, and their ecology is closely linked to access to woody cover and water. In hot months, they create wallows—mud depressions near ponds or marshy edges—which can become focal points for damage. citeturn33view3turn13search2  

Habitat  
Feral hogs use an enormous range of habitats, including timbered areas, brushlands, mesquite–grasslands, and riparian corridors. In Central Texas, creek bottoms and greenbelts provide both cover and water, making parks and preserves vulnerable—especially where hogs can move in from adjacent undeveloped tracts. citeturn33view3  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for fresh rooting in soft soils along creek banks, wet meadows, and low spots where water collects. Damage often appears overnight, and hogs may retreat into dense brush during daylight, making daytime sightings less common than signs. citeturn33view3

Feral hogs are one of the most disruptive invasive mammals in Texas. Their rooting disturbs soil and vegetation, can shift plant succession, reduces plant species richness in heavily disturbed patches, and competes with native wildlife for foods such as mast (acorns). They also create erosion risks along waterways and can spread diseases of concern for livestock and potentially people. citeturn33view3turn13search2

- Competes with native wildlife for acorns and other key foods

House Mouse

Invasive / Introduced  

Description  
House mice are small, short-haired mice with moderately large ears and scaly tails. Their coloration varies but often appears ochraceous with darker suffusion above and paler below. House mice are widely distributed across Texas, usually close to humans—homes, sheds, stores, barns—but they can also occupy weedy field edges and watercourse vegetation where cover allows. Description  
Norway rats are large, heavy-bodied rats—chunkier than roof rats—with tails typically shorter than head-and-body length. Their size and robust build are key field marks. Black (roof) rats are slender, medium-sized rats with long scaly tails that are often as long as or longer than head-and-body length.
