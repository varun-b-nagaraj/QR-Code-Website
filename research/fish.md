Largemouth Bass  
Micropterus salmoides  

Native  

**Description:** Largemouth bass are sturdy, olive-green gamefish known for the large mouth that extends past the eye. Adults typically reach 12–18 inches (some up to 2 feet) and weigh several pounds. They have a broad, robust body with dark blotches forming a horizontal band along each side. Breeding behavior in spring creates visible nest beds in shallow water. Young bass grow rapidly in warm months and often develop spots as juveniles before filling in their pattern as they age.  

**Habitat:** These bass prefer warm, calm waters with plenty of cover. In Central Texas parks, they inhabit ponds, lakes, slow-moving river pools, and backwaters. They favor environments with submerged logs, vegetation, or rock shelters. Water clarity can vary; bass thrive in both clear and slightly turbid water, but they usually stay near structure whether it is vegetation or fallen trees. They do best in areas with moderate depths (6–15 feet in deeper lakes, or shaded pockets in creeks) where they can ambush prey.   

Where Found In The Park  
Largemouth bass are most likely found in quiet coves of park lakes, slow creek pools behind dams or rock bars, and deep holes along shaded banks. In spring, males can be seen guarding shallow sandy nests near pond edges or creek shoals. Trail signs might point out fishing piers or boathouses on park lakes where bass are often caught.  

**Ecological Role:** As top warmwater predators, largemouth bass help regulate populations of smaller fish (like sunfish, minnows, and young catfish) and aquatic invertebrates. By controlling these prey populations, they contribute to a balanced ecosystem and help prevent any one species from overpopulating. Bass also serve as an important food source for great blue herons, otters, and humans.  
- Preys on smaller fish, amphibians, and large aquatic insects, keeping those populations in check.  
- Creates nesting and feeding opportunities for other species (nesting bass stir up sediment, which some small fish exploit).  
- Supports recreational fishing—a reason many parks actively manage lake and creek habitats.  

Fun Fact  
- The largemouth bass is the most popular sportfish in Texas; anglers prize its fight and size.  
- A male largemouth bass guards the nest fiercely; people sometimes spot the male fanning a shallow depression where fry (baby fish) will hatch.  
- Largemouth bass can leap high when hooked, earning them the nickname “bucketmouth” for their huge jaws and acrobatic fights.  

Bluegill Sunfish  
Lepomis macrochirus  

Native  

**Description:** Bluegills are common sunfish with a round, flat body up to 6–10 inches long. They are typically blue or olive on top, with vertical dark bars on the sides and bright orange or yellow bellies. A distinctive black “ear” spot on the gill cover (operculum) and a black dot at the base of the tail help identify them. They develop quickly, and juveniles school together while adults tend to be solitary or in small groups. In summer, males create shallow gravel nests in colonies, often near docks or logs.  

**Habitat:** Bluegills prefer warm, well-oxygenated waters with abundant vegetation. In park ponds and creek pools, you’ll find them around lily pads, grasses, or fallen trees. They thrive over sandy or muddy bottoms where they can forage for insects and zooplankton. These fish tolerate a range of water clarities but usually stay in sunny shallows (1–6 feet deep) where their nest colonies get warmth. Plants and brush provide both food (insect prey) and hiding spots from predators.  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for bluegills in almost any calm pond or slow creek with cover. They often hover beneath overhanging banks or near aquatic plants along shorelines. In parks, bluegills show up around fishing platforms and can be seen scooting over sunlit flats in search of food. During nesting season (late spring to summer), watch for circular nests (shallow pits) in groups near grassy banks.  

**Ecological Role:** Bluegill sunfish feed on insects, plankton, and small crustaceans, linking the aquatic insect world to higher-level predators. They are a key prey species for larger fish (like bass and catfish) and for birds (herons, kingfishers). By grazing on insect larvae, they help control mosquito and pest populations.  
- Controls aquatic insect and plankton levels through grazing.  
- Feeds larger predators: bluegills are a main diet item for bass, catfish, and turtles.  
- Helps cycle nutrients; feeding stirs up the sediment, making nutrients available to aquatic plants.  

Fun Fact  
- Bluegills are sometimes called “bream” or “perch” by local anglers.  
- They can tolerate lower oxygen better than many fish and often show up even in small park ponds that get warm.  
- When startled, a school of bluegills makes a rapid zigzag motion—look like a shimmering blue cloud!  

Channel Catfish  
Ictalurus punctatus  

Native  

**Description:** Channel catfish are slender, silvery-gray to bluish fish with smooth skin and no scales. They have a deeply forked tail and a white or lightly spotted belly. Look for their four pairs of whisker-like barbels around the mouth (hence “catfish”) and a small adipose fin (a fleshy fin) on the back. Young channel cats grow fast; mature ones can reach 1–2 feet or more. They are active mostly at dawn and dusk (crepuscular) and sometimes at night.  

**Habitat:** Channel catfish inhabit a wide range of waters, from small streams to large reservoirs. They prefer deeper holes (5+ feet) during the day and often move into shallower water at night to feed. They are bottom-feeders, so water clarity is less critical, but they do best in habitats with plenty of food (insects, mollusks, smaller fish, detritus). Substrate type varies – they’re comfortable on sand, gravel, or mud – but they often shelter under fallen trees or riverbank undercuts.  

Where Found In The Park  
In parks with streams or lakes, channel catfish lurk in deep pools and under overhanging banks. They are less likely to be seen but often caught by fishermen using bait on or near the bottom. In shallower ponds, look near stumps or rocky edges after dark. Signs might mark “no fishing” zones to protect young catfish while they feed at night in shallow areas.  

**Ecological Role:** Channel catfish are important bottom-feeders and scavengers. They consume a variety of food — insects, crayfish, snails, smaller fish, and organic debris — and help clean up the ecosystem by eating dead material. They in turn support wildlife: many birds (e.g. herons, egrets, kingfishers) and otters feed on catfish, and a single adult catfish can contain parasites and nutrients that feed many other creatures when eaten.  
- Recycles nutrients by scavenging on the bottom, keeping waterways cleaner.  
- Provides sport and food fish resources for humans (contributing to wildlife-watching and fishing).  
- Serves as a prey item for wildlife (otters, raccoons, herons) that forage in creeks.  

Fun Fact  
- Channel catfish have the ability to “breath” by swallowing air at the surface, helping them survive in low-oxygen waters.  
- They can detect minute electrical signals from prey (using their whiskers) to hunt in murky water.  
- People often mistake their dark peppered spots for pollution; actually, those spots are normal and fade as the fish ages.  

Common Carp  
Cyprinus carpio  

Invasive  

**Description:** Common carp are large, heavy-bodied fish introduced from Europe. They can reach 2 feet or more and have bronze-gold scales that shimmer in the sun. Carp have fleshy lips (often seen foraging in mud) and a pair of small barbels (whisker-like feelers) near the mouth. They are omnivores: if you see one, it’s often with mouth down in the sediment, sucking up plants, insects, and small creatures. They can live a long time (over 20 years) and grow very large in warm, rich waters.  

**Habitat:** Carp thrive in ponds, lakes, and slow rivers, especially those with muddy bottoms. They prefer warm, nutrient-rich waters and can tolerate low oxygen. Carp often stir up bottom sediment while feeding, which increases water turbidity (cloudiness). In parks, carp are common where warm lakes or impoundments exist. They avoid fast currents, so look for them in backwaters, pond margins, and behind in-park dams.  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for common carp in shallow lake edges and slow backwaters. They often surface or come near shore when feeding – you might even hear a “gulping” sound. Parks with weedy ponds or fishing lakes often host carp; signs like “wildlife refuge” might note their presence. Young carp school in shallow weeds, while big carp wallow in deeper mud.  

**Ecological Role:** As a non-native species, carp have a mixed impact. They feed on aquatic plants and invertebrates, often reducing plant cover and muddying the water. This can harm native plants and fish that need clear water. However, they also serve as food for large predators (alligator gar, birds). In educational signage, carp are often used to discuss invasive species control and habitat restoration.  
- Dig up sediments and uproot plants, which can release nutrients and cause algae blooms (a negative effect on water quality).  
- Serve as large-bodied prey for fish-eating birds (e.g., herons, pelicans) and some big fish.  
- Can indicate ecosystem imbalance; their presence often leads parks to manage vegetation and water clarity.  

Fun Fact  
- Carp can breathe air from the surface, allowing them to survive in very low-oxygen water.  
- An ancient fish: carp have been farmed for thousands of years in Asia (they’re related to koi).  
- They can dramatically change a pond: sometimes carp are removed to help restore clear water and native plants.  

Grass Carp  
Ctenopharyngodon idella  

Invasive (Sterile Stocked)  

**Description:** Grass carp are large, torpedo-shaped fish similar in size to big carp (often 2–4 feet long). Their color is olive or gray, fading to white below. Unlike common carp, grass carp have no barbels (whiskers) and a more streamlined shape. They have a small head and large scales. They were introduced to eat aquatic plants, so if you see one, it’s likely near vegetation.  

**Habitat:** These fish favor vegetated ponds, lakes, or calm river areas. They need plenty of aquatic plants, which they eat constantly. Water conditions are less critical except that extreme cold can stress them; Central Texas stocks are usually “triploid” (sterile) to prevent breeding. In parks, they are found where milfoil, hydrilla, or other aquatic plants grow thickly. They avoid strong currents, so look in pond corners or near city reservoirs.  

Where Found In The Park  
Grass carp may be seen cruising along the edges of weedy ponds or lakes. They usually stay near their food, so places rich in submerged plants will hold them. Visitors might spot them at sunrise or sunset when fish sometimes come up shallow to feed on surface plants.  

**Ecological Role:** Stocked grass carp are used by wildlife managers to control excessive aquatic weeds. While they help reduce invasive plants, if unchecked they can remove too much vegetation, harming habitat. As fish, they are also food for larger predators. Their role in park education is often to explain intentional stocking and plant management.  
- Controls aquatic weeds by heavy grazing (often a management tool).  
- Provides big-fish forage for birds and sport fish (like big bass that eat grass carp).  
- Teaches about biological control: parks may use grass carp to clean up overgrown ponds naturally.  

Fun Fact  
- Most grass carp in Texas are triploid (sterile) so they eat plants but don’t reproduce in the wild.  
- A single grass carp can eat several pounds of aquatic plants each day—imagine a vacuum cleaner for the lake!  
- Because they are strong swimmers, they sometimes jump out of shallow water when startled.  

Blue Catfish  
Ictalurus furcatus  

Native  

**Description:** Blue catfish are among the largest North American catfish, often reaching 2–3 feet or more. They have a blue-gray color with white bellies and can have small black spots on young fish. Blue cats have four pairs of barbels and a dorsal fin without a spine (smooth). Unlike channel catfish, their tail is slightly rounded. They grow quickly in our warm lakes and are prized by anglers for their size.  

**Habitat:** These fish prefer large reservoirs and deep river pools. They often congregate in deep water (20+ feet), especially during hot summer days, surfacing at night or early morning to feed near shore. Blue catfish are hardy; they tolerate a wide range of conditions but prefer clearer water with structure (like rocks or submerged timber). In Central Texas, they thrive in big lakes and dams with stable, cool deep spots.  

Where Found In The Park  
In large park lakes or rivers, blue catfish hide in deep holes, ledges, and around submerged structures. Anglers often find them during night fishing or with deep-setlines. In daylight, look for them in the deepest dark areas or trailing schools near underwater dropoffs. They are less common in small ponds.  

**Ecological Role:** As top predators, blue catfish eat a variety of prey—small fish, mollusks, and even birds or mammals at the water’s edge. They help control invasive species (like Asian clams) but can also reduce native mussels and perch. Their large size and sport value mean they connect recreation with conservation; healthy catfish populations indicate good water management.  
- Consumes prey like shad, sunfish, and crabs, helping balance those populations.  
- Provides trophy fishing opportunities that encourage park conservation and lake cleanup.  
- Their feeding on invasive clams and mussels can actually help control those pests.  

Fun Fact  
- Blue catfish can live 20 years or more in good habitat (that’s older than many people in the park!).  
- Texas anglers have caught blue cats over 100 pounds on record; even a 50-pounder is a giant.  
- Baby blue catfish look a lot like small channel cats but lose their spots as they grow (adult blues are usually solid gray-blue).  

Flathead Catfish  
Pylodictis olivaris  

Native  

**Description:** Flathead catfish have a broad, flat head (hence the name) and a mottled brown body, which camouflages well against wood and rock. Adults often exceed 30 inches and are yellowish-brown with darker patchy bands. They have a single flap of skin under each side of the jaw, and unlike channel cats, flatheads have a square tail. Young flatheads have very large eyes compared to their head, giving them a characteristic deep-set gaze.  

**Habitat:** These fish prefer large rivers and reservoirs with deep, slow-moving water and lots of cover (log jams, undercut banks, deep holes). They are ambush predators, so they lay in wait near submerged structure, darting out to grab prey. In Texas, flatheads avoid the warmer shallows of summer heat, often staying at depth. Spawning occurs in the warm months in moderately shallow riverbackwaters or lake edges.  

Where Found In The Park  
Flatheads are most likely found in large creeks or river sections within parks—especially below dams or in spring-fed streams with consistent flow. They are usually deep or hidden, so anglers typically hook them around snags or in deep creek pools. You might see them near old pilings or rocky outcrops in the water, lying motionless.  

**Ecological Role:** Flathead catfish are top carnivores and feed on other fish (even perch and bass), crayfish, and other animals. They can strongly impact fish communities if abundant (they sometimes decline native sunfish or buffalo fish). However, they are natural parts of Texas rivers and indicate good habitat complexity when present. They also attract predators and scavengers—herons and bears have been known to prey on hooked flatheads.  
- Regulates prey fish (sunfish, shad) through predation.  
- Acts as an indicator of water quality and habitat; they need clean, well-oxygenated water to thrive.  
- Supports recreational fishing and food uses; their presence often signals a healthy river system.  

Fun Fact  
- Female flathead catfish guard their young, a rarity among Texas fish. They fan nests and chase away intruders.  
- Native Americans and early Texans respected flathead as a valuable food fish; even today their firm white meat is prized.  
- Flatheads will bite frogs, snakes, and other unusual prey—reportedly even rattlesnakes! Their ambush skill is uncanny.  

Black Crappie  
Pomoxis nigromaculatus  

Native  

**Description:** Black crappie are silvery fish with a compressed (almost round) body and dark mottling. They have seven or eight dorsal spines and a slightly upturned mouth. Adults grow to about 9–12 inches. Unlike white crappie (with 5–6 dorsal spines), blacks have a more irregular pattern of black spots all over a silvery-olive body. They often school tightly and can become very dense by summer.  

**Habitat:** Black crappie prefer clear, slow waters of ponds, lakes, and slow rivers. They especially like cover: brush piles, submerged timber, and thick vegetation. They spawn in spring over gravel or sand in 2–4 feet of water, often in sheltered bays. Crappies feed on smaller fish and invertebrates near the surface or mid-water. They do best in waters with good clarity (murkiness hinders their sight-feeding) and plenty of protective cover.  

Where Found In The Park  
In park lakes or ponds, look for crappie around fallen tree branches or artificial fish habitats. They school in deeper water during the day and come up in the shallows at night to feed. Anglers often find them by casting near docks or brush. Signs at fishing lakes may even show the special crappie-spot where underwater structures attract the schools.  

**Ecological Role:** Crappie are mid-level predators. Their feeding helps keep smaller baitfish (like minnows) in check. In turn, they are a favorite prey of larger fish (bass, catfish) and birds (cormorants, herons). By schooling tightly, crappie also support sport fishing, linking ecological health to recreation.  
- Regulates small fish populations through schooling predation.  
- Supports game fish and birds by serving as a forage species.  
- Contributes to nutrient cycling: large schools can quickly alter plankton levels in summer.  

Fun Fact  
- In some Central Texas lakes, black crappie replace whites or mix; their mottled look makes them camouflaged when schooling.  
- Crappie can tolerate cooler water than many sunfish, which is why they thrive in spring-fed creek ponds.  
- Nighttime crappie “feeding frenzies” near lights are a popular spectacle for fishermen: the schools come shallow to eat insects.  

Spotted Gar  
Lepisosteus oculatus  

Native  

**Description:** Spotted gar are long, cylindrical fish with sharp, tooth-lined jaws. They reach 2–3 feet long in our area. Their backs are olive-green and heavily spotted with dark spots that continue onto the elongated snout. The belly is pale. Unlike their larger cousin (alligator gar), spotted gar are slimmer with a more pointed snout. They breathe air from the surface, which lets them live in low-oxygen waters.  

**Habitat:** Spotted gar inhabit slow bays, oxbow lakes, backwaters, and weedy pockets of rivers. They prefer warm, quiet water and are often found near submerged vegetation or woody debris. Soil and substrate matter little to them; what’s crucial is cover and slow flow. They will sit nearly motionless in shallow water, waiting to ambush prey (fish, frogs). Unlike most fish, gar can spend time at the surface gulping air, so they do well in summer ponds that might get low on oxygen.  

Where Found In The Park  
Spotted gar are often found in the shallow, marshy parts of park lakes or slow creek backwaters—places where cattails or lily pads grow. They can be seen near the surface or hiding below logs. During low water, they might strand in isolated pools. Signs in fish-focused parks may point out these “living fossils” in the fish community.  

**Ecological Role:** Spotted gar are top ambush predators in their habitat. They help control populations of chubs, shiners, and small fish. By preying on weaker individuals, they keep fish communities healthier. Gar also serve as indicators of habitat quality: their presence suggests a healthy vegetated zone. They are rarely eaten by other animals due to their tough scales and strong jaws, but large birds and alligators (in other parts of Texas) will take them occasionally.  
- Controls small fish numbers, aiding in balance between species.  
- Shows visitors an ancient lineage (gars have existed unchanged for millions of years).  
- Oxygenating behavior (surface breathing) reminds park users how fish adapt to habitat conditions.  

Fun Fact  
- Spotted gar’s bony scales are so hard, they were once used as arrowheads by Native peoples!  
- They sometimes leap out of the water when startled, so observers might catch a glimpse of their spotted bodies in flight.  
- Biologists call gars “living fossils” because they’ve looked similar since dinosaur times. Seeing one connects us to very ancient ecosystems.  

Florida Longear Sunfish  
Lepomis humilis  

Native  

**Description:** Florida longear sunfish are small, colorful sunfish with a body shape like bluegills but a longer opercular flap (“ear”) edged with bright orange. They grow to about 4–5 inches. Their body is green to bluish with orange below, and vertical bars faint on the sides. The elongated flap on the gill cover can be a field mark. They are less common than bluegills and usually smaller.  

**Habitat:** These sunfish live in clear, spring-fed creeks, shallow lakes, and small ponds—especially where water is cool and oxygen-rich. They prefer gravel or sandy bottoms and abundant aquatic plants or brush. Unlike bluegill, longears do better in slightly cooler water, so in spring-fed parks you may find them year-round. They eat small invertebrates and fry of other fish.  

Where Found In The Park  
Check shallow, slow sections of creeks and spring ponds with lots of vegetation. They are often in small schools near the bottom or around submerged branches. Longears are shy; look for quick bursts of movement in clear water around submerged plants.  

**Ecological Role:** Longear sunfish, like other small sunfish, help control insect populations by feeding on aquatic larvae. They are prey for bigger fish and wading birds. Since they thrive in spring creeks, their presence indicates high water quality. They also contribute to the park’s biodiversity by adding variety to the sunfish community.  
- Grazes on insects and crustaceans, helping manage aquatic invertebrate levels.  
- Serves as food for larger bass and herons.  
- Supports river health: they often appear only in clean, well-oxygenated waters.  

Fun Fact  
- The name “longear” comes from that long orange edge on the gill flap—it’s easy to remember once you see it.  
- Longears are more common in springs and spring-fed streams; seeing them in a park suggests a stable water source.  
- Though called “Florida” longear, they’re native here too—they range into Central Texas!  

Mexican Mosquitofish  
Gambusia affinis  

Invasive  

**Description:** Mosquitofish are tiny, quick-moving minnows, usually 1.5–2 inches long. Females have a rounded belly, and males are smaller with a pointed anal fin (used in reproduction). They are gray-green with a mottled pattern. These hard-to-spot fish have large fins relative to body size and a noticeable upturned mouth. Mosquitofish are livebearers (giving birth to free-swimming young) and breed year-round in warm conditions.  

**Habitat:** Introduced to eat mosquito larvae, mosquitofish now live in many parks’ still waters—especially shallow ponds, ditches, and slow creeks. They tolerate a wide range of conditions including very warm, low-oxygen water. Typically found in sunny, weedy shallows, they can survive conditions that other fish can’t (for example, when a pond dries partly they can survive in mud).  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for them in sunny margins of ponds, small lakes, or drainage ditches. They often form dense schools near the surface or among submerged vegetation. Since they eat mosquito larvae, some parks deliberately keep them in water gardens or storm ponds—signs may even advertise this “natural mosquito control.”  

**Ecological Role:** Mosquitofish eat mosquito larvae (and other small invertebrates), which can reduce mosquito numbers. However, they also compete with and eat tiny native fish and insect larvae, sometimes harming local biodiversity. In interpretation, they illustrate both biological control and invasive species lessons.  
- Consumes mosquito larvae—hence the name—but also preys on many native aquatic insects and fish fry.  
- Supports food webs by providing quick meals for small predators (big fish, waterbirds).  
- Indicates human impact: their presence shows how people alter ecosystems (for better and worse).  

Fun Fact  
- Despite their name, mosquitofish aren’t picky; they eat almost anything tiny they can fit in their mouths.  
- They were introduced worldwide for mosquito control and are now one of the world’s most widespread freshwater fish (often unwittingly).  
- In some parks, naturalists challenge visitors to spot a mosquitofish in a “mosquito habitat” as a fun game (they’re quite camouflaged until they dart).  

Rio Grande Cichlid  
Herichthys cyanoguttatus  

Invasive  

**Description:** The Rio Grande cichlid is a chunky, aggressive fish with a greenish body covered in olive-green spots. Adults reach about 8–10 inches. They have a pointed head and a distinct “nape” hump in older males. Their jaws are robust; when not breeding, they are often solid greenish-tan, but during courtship their colors deepen and vertical bands become visible. They breathe air when needed by gulping at the surface.  

**Habitat:** Native to the Rio Grande basin, they have been introduced to Texas ponds and streams. They prefer slow-moving water with structure — rocks, logs, brush. They also tolerate brackish and warm water. In Central Texas, they survive cold winters (unlike some other cichlids) and can live in ponds, drainages, and canals. They dominate the habitat, often displacing native sunfish.  

Where Found In The Park  
Look for cichlids in overgrown ponds and weedy creek sections, especially where water warms up. They tend to stay near cover — under vegetation or around submerged structure — and can often be seen in pairs defending territory. Signs might note “aggressive cichlid” if they are stocked or known to occur.  

**Ecological Role:** As a non-native predator, Rio Grande cichlids can drastically alter local fish communities. They eat native fish fry and compete with bass and sunfish. However, they also clean up detritus and insects. In parks, they serve as a cautionary tale: once established, they are nearly impossible to remove. They also provide a unique “alien fish” to intrigue visitors and discuss habitat changes.  
- Preys on smaller fish and invertebrates, often reducing native sunfish and minnow populations.  
- Provides a food source for larger native fish and raccoons or herons (though its spines can be a deterrent).  
- Creates complex breeding territories (digging pits), which can change sediment patterns in shallow areas.  

Fun Fact  
- Rio Grande cichlids are one of the northernmost members of the cichlid family, surviving winter chills in Texas.  
- Their aggressive “Defender of the Realm” nickname among anglers comes from how fiercely they guard their nests.  
- If a visitor sees an “aggressive green spotted fish,” it’s likely this cichlid — which didn’t get to Texas on its own (people released them).  

Bowfin  
Amia calva  

Native  

**Description:** Bowfin are long, cylindrical fish with a distinctive dorsal fin running nearly the length of their back. They are often brownish or olive, with darker mottling. Their large eyes sit well forward, and they have a broad mouth with sharp teeth. Adults average 1–2 feet. Bowfin are ancient survivors; their body shape is reminiscent of prehistoric fish. They will sometimes gulp air at the surface and create ripples – a unique behavior among Texas fish.  

**Habitat:** Bowfin prefer weedy, swampy areas of lakes and slow rivers — the same habitats where gar and large sunfish occur. They tolerate low oxygen by breathing air, which lets them live in swampy backwaters where other predators can’t easily follow. They hide in vegetation by day and hunt by ambush. In Central Texas parks, they can be found in old oxbows, vegetated pond edges, or quiet bayous.  

Where Found In The Park  
You’re most likely to spot bowfin in dense cover near shorelines — around lily pads, duckweed mats, or brush in slow ponds. Because they surface to breathe, fishermen sometimes hear them gulp air. In spring, you may see bowfin nests: males fan out circular nest beds in shallow water like giant sunfish.  

**Ecological Role:** As top predators in marshy waters, bowfin help control populations of fish like shad, sunfish, and small catfish. Their presence indicates a rich, swampy habitat. They are eaten by very few animals due to their tough gular plates and aggressive nature, but during their nest period, larger predators may feed on unwary fry. They also historically fed the settlers (milkers) who ate them, so they have cultural importance as a “bread and butter” fish for early North Americans.  
- Regulates prey fish in vegetation-choked habitats, keeping smaller fish populations healthy.  
- Supports nutrient recycling through nest-building activities and as prey (especially their eggs and fry) for other wildlife.  
- Serves as a living link to ancient ecosystems (a “prehistoric” fish in modern parks).  

Fun Fact  
- Bowfin are sometimes called “mudfish” or “pond pike.” Their ability to breathe air lets them survive in muddy, low-water conditions.  
- Early settlers believed bowfin broth had medicinal properties, leading to another nickname: “doctorfish.”  
- They can ambush prey with a lightning strike, puffing out their gills to lure prey close — a hunting trick.  

Neosho Madtom (Stonecat)  
Noturus placidus  

Native  

**Description:** Madtoms are small catfish (4–6 inches) with flattened heads and a stinging spine in their pectoral fin. The Neosho madtom is mottled brown and slender. It prefers hiding under rocks and debris. It has a whiskered face like other catfish but is notably smaller than the catfish people typically fish for.  

**Habitat:** This species lives in clear, rocky streams and springs. It hides under flat stones in moderate current. They are often found in riffles and pools with gravel or bedrock bottoms. In parks, look for them in spring-fed creeks or cool canyon streams with good water quality.  

Where Found In The Park  
Park visitors rarely see madtoms, but they inhabit the same creeks where you might wade or rock-hop. Look for them under rocks in clear runs, especially where water stays cold and flowing. Signage might encourage leaving such creeks undisturbed, as they hide under rocks by day.  

**Ecological Role:** As bottom-dwelling insectivores, madtoms help control aquatic insect larvae. They are also indicators of pristine conditions: their presence often means a stream is very clean. Because they are secretive and small, they mainly serve as prey for larger fish and wading birds. Their limited range in Texas makes them of conservation concern.  
- Feeds on insects and crustaceans along stream bottoms, aiding nutrient breakdown.  
- Serves as prey for larger stream predators (bass, herons).  
- Acts as an indicator species: a sign of unpolluted, cold-water habitat when present.  

Fun Fact  
- Madtoms can deliver a painful sting if handled — a reminder of their sharp pectoral spines.  
- Because of their secretive nature, finding a madtom in a park stream is a lucky wildlife spotting for enthusiasts.  
- Their population in Texas is limited; conservation efforts focus on protecting spring streams where they live.
