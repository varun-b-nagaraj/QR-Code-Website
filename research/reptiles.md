## Habitat crosswalk for Central Texas parks

Central Texas park systems around the Austin/Cedar Park region sit near a major ecological boundary: the transition from the Blackland Prairie (fertile, heavy clay soils and historically grassland-dominated landscapes) to the Edwards Plateau / Hill Country (limestone-based uplands with oak–juniper woodlands and rocky slopes). citeturn9search1turn9search0turn9search6 This boundary creates high reptile diversity because visitors can move from prairie openings to limestone outcrops to riparian creek corridors within a short walk, and reptiles track these microhabitats closely. citeturn9search8turn9search9turn9search0

## Safety and respectful viewing in parks

Venomous snakes in Texas fall into four groups: coral snakes, copperheads, cottonmouths, and rattlesnakes, and Texas Parks & Wildlife emphasizes that most bites result from people taking unnecessary risks—especially attempting to handle or harass snakes. citeturn26view0turn0search9 The safest and most educational approach for signage is consistent: keep a respectful distance, stay on trails, watch where you place hands and feet (especially in leaf litter and rock piles), and never try to pick up any snake—venomous or not. citeturn26view0  

From an education perspective, a few field habits reduce both fear and risk: scan sunny trail edges ahead of you, step onto logs rather than over them when possible, and treat creek banks and thick shoreline vegetation as “snake habitat zones” where visibility is lower. citeturn26view0 If a bite ever occurs, Texas Parks & Wildlife directs readers to CDC guidance for symptoms and first aid, reinforcing that professional medical care is essential rather than folk remedies. citeturn26view0
